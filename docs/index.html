Optimizing the Optimizers: Measuring Learned Control of MIP Solvers on a Single DGX Spark

Berk Orbay · technical report, 24 September 2026 · doi:10.5281/zenodo.22947442

The default settings of a mixed-integer programming (MIP) solver are tuned to do well on average; a user with a fixed time budget cares about the instance at hand. On one NVIDIA DGX Spark we measure, for HiGHS, SCIP and NVIDIA cuOpt, how much sooner a good solution could be found by choosing settings per instance, whether that gain comes from the initial choice or from switching settings during the solve, and what simple and learned controllers recover, with their decision time charged to the budget. The measure is the primal integral, the gap to the best known solution averaged over the budget.

Main findings

Over 8,000 runs on MIPLIB 2017, two ML4CO families and PGLib-UC unit commitment:

  1. How much there is to gain. Choosing the best of six configurations per instance would lower the primal integral by 19 % over all 336 attempted instances, 25 % on the 313 where some configuration finds a solution and 41 % on MIPLIB (one seed).
  2. Choosing, not switching. Inside SCIP most of the gain comes from the initial choice: the right static setting is worth 12 % (45 instances) and 20 % (the 20 with the most headroom), while the six switching schedules we tried add about 2 % at 120 s and nothing detectable at 300 s.
  3. A timing-matched control. On held-out ML4CO instances one fixed setting improves on SCIP's defaults by 33 %. An untrained local language-model agent makes the same first move in most runs, but the fixed setting applied at the same solver callback beats it on 22 of 30 instances.
